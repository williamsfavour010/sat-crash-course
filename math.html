Algebra

≈35% of the test · Patterns 1–4 · The biggest lever — master these first

The intuition

Every linear equation is a starting amount plus a rate. In y = mx + b, the slope m is the rate — how fast y changes per unit of x. The intercept b is the starting value — what y is when x = 0, before anything happens. Once you see which number is "the rate" and which is "the head start," word problems turn into fill-in-the-blank.

Simple

A plumber charges a $45 service fee plus $60 per hour of work. The cost C for h hours is C = 60h + 45. What do the 60 and the 45 represent?

45 = the flat fee (intercept); 60 = the hourly rate (slope).
  1. 45 stands alone — it's the cost when h = 0: the fee charged before any work happens. That's b, the y-intercept.
  2. 60 multiplies the variable — it's the cost added per hour. That's m, the rate of change.
Mid

A phone plan charges a flat $25 activation fee plus $12 per GB of data. Write the equation for total cost C given g GB, and find the cost for 8 GB.

C = 12g + 25; 8 GB costs $121.
  1. $25 is charged once, before any data — it's the starting value: C = ?g + 25.
  2. $12 multiplies each GB — it's the rate: C = 12g + 25.
  3. C(8) = 12(8) + 25 = 96 + 25 = 121.
Stretch

The graph of a linear function f passes through (3, 11) and (7, 27). What is f(10)?

f(10) = 39
  1. Slope m = (rise)/(run) = 27 − 117 − 3 = 4. Every +1 in x adds 4 to f.
  2. From x = 7 to x = 10 is +3 steps: f(10) = 27 + 4(3) = 39.

Check via the full equation: b = 11 − 4(3) = −1, so f(x) = 4x − 1 and f(10) = 40 − 1 = 39 ✓

The can't-unsee-it
In y = mx + b, m is the rate per unit and b is the value at zero. Every linear word problem is asking you to spot those two numbers.
The SAT's favorite trap
"What does the 45 represent?" with the rate offered as a distractor. If a number multiplies the variable, it's the rate; if it stands alone, it's the starting value. Never mix them.
The intuition

Slope is direction. Parallel lines walk the same direction → identical slopes. Perpendicular lines turn 90° → the slope becomes the negative reciprocal (flip the fraction, flip the sign). The SAT hides this in standard form Ax + By = C, where you must solve for y first to see the slope.

Simple

What is the slope of a line perpendicular to y = 2x + 5?

−1/2
  1. Slope is 2 = 2/1. Flip → 1/2; negate → −1/2.
Mid

Write the equation of a line parallel to y = 3x − 2 that passes through the point (1, 5).

y = 3x + 2
  1. Parallel → same slope: m = 3.
  2. Through (1, 5): y − 5 = 3(x − 1) → y = 3x + 2. (At x = 1: y = 5 ✓)
Stretch

Line L has equation 3x + 4y = 12. A line perpendicular to L passes through (2, 1). What is its y-intercept?

−5/3
  1. Solve L for y: 4y = −3x + 12 → y = −(3/4)x + 3. Slope of L is −3/4.
  2. Perpendicular slope = negative reciprocal = 4/3.
  3. Through (2, 1): y − 1 = (4/3)(x − 2). Set x = 0: y = 1 + (4/3)(−2) = 1 − 8/3 = −5/3.
The can't-unsee-it
Parallel = same slope. Perpendicular = negative reciprocal (flip the fraction, flip the sign). In standard form, isolate y first.
The SAT's favorite trap
Forgetting to flip the sign (perpendicular to −3/4 is 4/3, not −4/3). And reading slope straight off Ax + By = C as if it were m — always solve for y first.
The intuition

Two equations, two unknowns = two graphs. The solution (x, y) is the single point where they cross — the one point that makes both true. Solve by elimination (add/subtract to kill a variable) or substitution. And on the digital SAT, Desmos trivializes these: graph both, click the intersection.

Simple

Solve: 2x + y = 7 and x − y = 2.

x = 3, y = 1
  1. Add the equations: the y's cancel → 3x = 9 → x = 3.
  2. Plug back into x − y = 2: 3 − y = 2 → y = 1.

Check: 2(3) + 1 = 7 ✓

Mid

Solve by substitution: y = 2x + 1 and 3x + y = 11.

x = 2, y = 5
  1. Substitute y = 2x + 1 into the second: 3x + 2x + 1 = 11 → 5x = 10 → x = 2.
  2. Then y = 2(2) + 1 = 5. (Check: 3(2) + 5 = 11 ✓)
Stretch

For what value of k does this system have NO solution? 2x + 3y = 7 and kx + 6y = 10.

k = 4
  1. "No solution" = the lines never meet = parallel = same left side, different right side.
  2. kx + 6y must be a multiple of 2x + 3y: comparing coefficients, k/2 = 6/3 → k/2 = 2 → k = 4.
  3. Check: with k = 4, doubling the first gives 4x + 6y = 14 ≠ 10 → parallel and distinct → no solution ✓.

If the right side had also been 14, it'd be the same line → infinitely many solutions. Same left, different right = zero; same left, same right = infinite.

The can't-unsee-it
Solution = intersection point. No solution = parallel (same slope, different intercept). Infinite = same line. Read the relationship before you start solving.
The SAT's favorite trap
"How many solutions?" questions punish anyone who starts grinding algebra first. Check the relationship between the equations — it takes five seconds.
The intuition

Most linear equations have exactly one solution. But sometimes the variable cancels entirely: you get either a true statement (6 = 6 → every x works, infinitely many solutions) or a false one (3 = 7 → no x works). The SAT loves these edge cases because they test whether you understand what an equation means. And when the test says an equation "is true for all x," you barely need algebra at all — pick any x (like 1 or 0) and plug it in.

Simple

How many solutions does 3(2x − 4) = 5x + 7 have?

Exactly one: x = 19
  1. Expand: 6x − 12 = 5x + 7 → x = 19.

The variable survives → one solution. Check: 3(38 − 4) = 102 = 5(19) + 7 ✓

Mid

How many solutions does 4(x + 1) = 4x + 5 have?

None
  1. Expand: 4x + 4 = 4x + 5. The x's cancel → 4 = 5, which is false.
  2. False statement = no value of x works. Zero solutions.
Stretch

For what value of a is 2(ax − 3) = 4x − 6 true for all values of x?

a = 2
  1. Expand: 2ax − 6 = 4x − 6. For this to hold for every x, the x-coefficients must match: 2a = 4 → a = 2. (The −6 = −6 already matches.)
  2. Shortcut check: plug x = 1 with a = 2: 2(2 − 3) = −2; 4 − 6 = −2 ✓
The can't-unsee-it
Variable cancels → look at what's left: true statement = infinite solutions, false = none. "True for all x" → just plug in a number.
The SAT's favorite trap
Distributing wrong — e.g. turning 2(ax − 3) into 2ax − 3. Slow down on the expansion step; that's where these are won or lost.

Advanced Math

≈35% of the test · Patterns 5–10 · Where 700 becomes 800

The intuition

Every quadratic is the same parabola dressed three ways, and each costume gives away a different secret for free: factored form y = a(x − r₁)(x − r₂) → the x-intercepts (roots) are r₁ and r₂; vertex form y = a(x − h)² + k → the vertex (max/min point) is (h, k); standard form y = ax² + bx + c → the y-intercept is c. Bonus truths: the axis of symmetry is x = (r₁ + r₂)/2 (midpoint of the roots); cup ∪ = a > 0, cap ∩ = a < 0. Every hard quadratic question is just asking you to change costumes.

Simple

For f(x) = (x − 3)(x + 5), find the vertex.

Vertex: (−1, −16) — a minimum
  1. Factored form → roots at x = 3 and x = −5.
  2. Axis of symmetry = midpoint = (3 + (−5))/2 = −1.
  3. Plug in: f(−1) = (−4)(4) = −16. Opens upward (a = 1 > 0), so this is the minimum ✓
Mid

A ball's height after t seconds is h(t) = −16t² + 64t + 5. What is the maximum height?

69 feet
  1. a = −16 < 0 → cap shape → the vertex is the maximum.
  2. Vertex t-value: t = −b/(2a) = −64/(2·(−16)) = 2.
  3. h(2) = −16(4) + 64(2) + 5 = −64 + 128 + 5 = 69.
Stretch

The line 2y = 4.5 intersects the parabola y = −4x² + bx, where b is a positive constant, at exactly one point. What is b?

b = 6
  1. Line: y = 2.25. Substitute: 2.25 = −4x² + bx → 4x² − bx + 2.25 = 0.
  2. "Exactly one intersection" = the quadratic has exactly one solution = discriminant equals zero.
  3. b² − 4(4)(2.25) = 0 → b² = 36 → b = ±6. Since b is positive: 6.
The can't-unsee-it
Factored → roots. Vertex form → max/min. Standard → y-intercept. Cup ∪ = positive, cap ∩ = negative. Hard questions = translate between costumes.
The SAT's favorite trap
"Exactly one point/solution" is code for discriminant = 0 (see Pattern 6). Also: ± — always read whether the constant is specified positive or negative.
The intuition

For ax² + bx + c = 0, the number b² − 4ac interrogates the equation and reports back: positive = two distinct real solutions (parabola crosses the x-axis twice), zero = exactly one (touches once), negative = none (floats entirely above or below). You never need the full quadratic formula to answer "how many solutions?" — just the lie detector.

Simple

How many real solutions does x² − 5x + 6 = 0 have?

Two real solutions
  1. b² − 4ac = 25 − 24 = 1 > 0 → two real solutions. (Indeed x = 2, 3 ✓)
Mid

For what value of k does x² − 6x + k = 0 have exactly one real solution?

k = 9
  1. "Exactly one" → discriminant = 0: 36 − 4k = 0 → k = 9.
  2. Check: x² − 6x + 9 = (x − 3)² = 0 → one repeated root, x = 3 ✓
Stretch

For how many integer values of k does x² + kx + 9 = 0 have NO real solutions?

11 integer values
  1. No real solutions → discriminant < 0: k² − 4(1)(9) < 0 → k² < 36 → −6 < k < 6.
  2. Integers strictly between −6 and 6: −5, −4, …, 5 → 11 values.

At k = ±6 the discriminant is 0 → exactly one solution — so the inequality is strict. That's the whole question.

The can't-unsee-it
b² − 4ac > 0 → two solutions; = 0 → one; < 0 → none. "Exactly one" vs "none" = non-strict vs strict inequality.
The SAT's favorite trap
Sign errors in −4ac (especially when c is negative), and forgetting that "no real solutions" excludes the discriminant = 0 case.
The intuition

The single most tested idea in this domain: a linear function adds the same amount each step (constant difference); an exponential function multiplies by the same factor each step (constant ratio). Given a table, check differences first, then ratios — the answer announces itself. In formulas: linear is y = mx + b, exponential is y = abˣ (or a(1 + r)ˣ for growth rate r). Percent growth is multiplication in disguise: "grows by 7% per year" = ×1.07 each year.

Simple

x: 0, 1, 2, 3 → y: 5, 10, 20, 40. Linear or exponential?

Exponential: y = 5(2ˣ)
  1. Differences: 5, 10, 20 — not constant. Not linear.
  2. Ratios: 10/5 = 2, 20/10 = 2, 40/20 = 2 — constant! Exponential with factor 2, starting at 5.

Check: 5·2³ = 40 ✓

Mid

A savings account earns 5% interest per year, compounded annually. You deposit $1,000. Write the balance function for t years, and find the balance after 3 years.

B(t) = 1000(1.05)ᵗ; after 3 years: $1,157.63
  1. "Grows by 5% per year" = multiply by 1 + 0.05 = 1.05 each year → B(t) = 1000(1.05)ᵗ.
  2. B(3) = 1000(1.05)³ = 1000(1.157625) = $1,157.63.
Stretch

A bacteria culture starts at 200 cells and triples every hour. After how many hours will it reach 48,600 cells?

5 hours
  1. 200 · 3ᵗ = 48{,}600 → 3ᵗ = 243.
  2. 243 = 3⁵ → t = 5 hours.

"Triples" = ×3 (it grows by 200%, grows to 300% — same thing). "Increases by 300%" would be ×4. Percent language is the trap, not the math.

The can't-unsee-it
Table → check differences, then ratios. "Grows by r%" = multiply by (1 + r). "Triples" = ×3, not ×2.
The SAT's favorite trap
"Increased by 150%" means ×2.5, not ×1.5. And "doubles every day" from a day-1 value V means day d has V·2ᵈ⁻¹ — off-by-one errors on the exponent are the classic distractor.
The intuition

Every exponent question is built from five moves: (1) xᵃ·xᵇ = xᵃ⁺ᵇ, (2) xᵃ / xᵇ = xᵃ⁻ᵇ, (3) (xᵃ)ᵇ = xᵃᵇ, (4) x⁻ᵃ = 1/xᵃ, (5) xᵃᐟᵇ = ᵇ√(xᵃ) — fractional exponent means root. The hard ones just ask you to rewrite everything with the same base (usually 2, 3, or 10) — then it's arithmetic.

Simple

Simplify (2x³)² · x⁻¹.

4x⁵
  1. (2x³)² = 4x⁶ (move 3: power of a power multiplies exponents; don't forget the 2!).
  2. 4x⁶ · x⁻¹ = 4x⁶⁻¹ = 4x⁵ (moves 1 + 4).
Mid

Simplify (3x⁴)² / x³.

9x⁵
  1. Expand the power: (3x⁴)² = 9x⁸.
  2. Divide same bases → subtract exponents: 9x⁸ / x³ = 9x⁵.
Stretch

Which expression is equivalent to 8²ˣ / 4ˣ?

2⁴ˣ (equivalently 16ˣ)
  1. Same base (2): 8²ˣ = (2³)²ˣ = 2⁶ˣ; 4ˣ = (2²)ˣ = 2²ˣ.
  2. 2⁶ˣ / 2²ˣ = 2⁶ˣ⁻²ˣ = 2⁴ˣ = 16ˣ.
The can't-unsee-it
Five moves; when stuck, rewrite everything as powers of the same small base (2, 3, 10) — then subtract/add exponents.
The SAT's favorite trap
(x + y)² is not x² + y². Exponents distribute over multiplication, never over addition. Also: xᵃ + xᵇ does not combine — only multiplication/division of same bases.
The intuition

"Which expression is equivalent?" really means: can you see through the disguise? The disguises are always the same: FOIL it out, or factor it back — especially difference of squares a² − b² = (a−b)(a+b), perfect squares, and plain GCF. Always factor out the greatest common factor first; it makes everything smaller and the pattern visible.

Simple

Factor x² − 9.

(x − 3)(x + 3)
  1. Difference of squares: x² − 3² = (x − 3)(x + 3).
Mid

Which expression is equivalent to 2x(x + 5) − (x + 5)(x − 3)?

x² + 8x + 15
  1. FOIL both: 2x² + 10x − (x² − 3x + 5x − 15).
  2. Distribute the minus: 2x² + 10x − x² − 2x + 15 = x² + 8x + 15.

Verify quickly: at x = 1, original = 2(1)(6) − (6)(−2) = 24; answer = 1 + 8 + 15 = 24 ✓

Stretch

The equation (x + 2)(x − 5) = ax² + bx + c is true for all x, where a, b, c are constants. What is a + b + c?

−12
  1. Expand: x² − 5x + 2x − 10 = x² − 3x − 10 → a = 1, b = −3, c = −10.
  2. a + b + c = 1 − 3 − 10 = −12.

Speed-run: "true for all x" → plug in x = 1: (3)(−4) = −12 = a + b + c. Ten seconds.

The can't-unsee-it
Equivalent = FOIL out or factor back; GCF first. "True for all x" → plug in x = 1 (or 0).
The SAT's favorite trap
Sign errors when FOILing (the −5x + 2x step), and doing full algebra when the plug-in shortcut exists.
The intuition

To kill a square root, square both sides; to kill a denominator, multiply through. But both moves are one-way doors: squaring can create extraneous solutions — phantom answers that satisfy the squared equation but not the original. So the pattern is always two steps: solve, then check every answer in the original equation. The SAT deliberately includes the extraneous value as an answer choice.

Simple

Solve √(2x + 6) = 4.

x = 5
  1. Square: 2x + 6 = 16 → 2x = 10 → x = 5.
  2. Check: √16 = 4 ✓.
Mid

Solve √x = x − 6.

x = 9 only (x = 4 is extraneous)
  1. Square: x = x² − 12x + 36 → x² − 13x + 36 = 0 → (x − 9)(x − 4) = 0 → x = 9 or x = 4.
  2. Interrogate in the original: x = 9 → 3 = 9 − 6 ✓. x = 4 → 2 = 4 − 6 = −2 ✗ — phantom, kill it.
Stretch

What is the solution to √(x + 5) = x − 1?

x = 4 only
  1. Square: x + 5 = (x − 1)² = x² − 2x + 1 → x² − 3x − 4 = 0 → (x − 4)(x + 1) = 0 → x = 4 or x = −1.
  2. Interrogate: x = 4 → √9 = 3 = 4 − 1 ✓. x = −1 → √4 = 2 ≠ −2 ✗ — extraneous, kill it.

−1 will absolutely be sitting there as choice B. That's why you check.

The can't-unsee-it
After squaring or clearing denominators, plug each candidate back into the original equation. The fake answer is always one of the choices.
The SAT's favorite trap
Stopping after solving. The check isn't optional — it is the question.

Problem-Solving & Data Analysis

≈15% of the test · Patterns 11–16 · Easy points, if you read carefully

The intuition

Two formulas run this whole pattern family: "p% of N" = (p/100)·N (of = ×), and percent change = (new − old)/old × 100%. The SAT's favorite trick is successive percent changes: a 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does not return to the start — percents multiply, they don't add.

Simple

A $40 shirt is marked up to $50. What is the percent increase?

25%
  1. (50 − 40)/40 = 10/40 = 0.25 = 25%. Old price is the denominator, always.
Mid

A value increases by 25% to reach 150. What was the original value?

120
  1. Let the original be x: a 25% increase means you now have 125% of x: 1.25x = 150.
  2. x = 150/1.25 = 120. (Check: 120 × 1.25 = 150 ✓)
Stretch

A store raises all prices by 20%, then applies a 20%-off coupon. What does a $100 item cost now?

$96 — not $100
  1. 100 × 1.20 = 120; then 120 × 0.80 = 96.
  2. The second 20% is taken off the bigger number. General truth: ×1.2 × 0.8 = ×0.96. Stacked percents multiply.
The can't-unsee-it
Percent change = (new − old)/old. Successive changes multiply — never add or cancel them.
The SAT's favorite trap
"Increased by 200%" = ×3 (you keep the original 100% and add 200%). Distractors always include the ×2 answer.
The intuition

A ratio is a recipe: 3 cups flour : 2 cups sugar works at any scale as long as you multiply everything by the same number. Unit conversion is the same idea — multiply by a clever form of 1 (like 5280 ft / 1 mi) so the unwanted units cancel. If the units don't cancel to what you want, you set the fraction upside down.

Simple

A recipe uses 3 cups of flour for every 2 cups of sugar. With 9 cups of flour, how much sugar?

6 cups
  1. 9 is 3 × 3, so scale everything by 3: 2 × 3 = 6.
Mid

The ratio of boys to girls in a class is 5:4, and there are 27 students total. How many boys?

15 boys
  1. 5 + 4 = 9 "parts"; 27/9 = 3 students per part.
  2. Boys: 5 × 3 = 15. (Girls: 4 × 3 = 12; 15 + 12 = 27 ✓)
Stretch

A car travels at 60 miles per hour. What is its speed in feet per second?

88 ft/s
  1. 60 mi/hr × (5280 ft / 1 mi) × (1 hr / 3600 s).
  2. = (60 × 5280)/3600 = 316800/3600 = 88.

The units guide you: miles cancel, hours cancel, feet/second remain. If they don't, flip the fraction.

The can't-unsee-it
Scale every part of a ratio by the same factor. Convert units by multiplying by fractions equal to 1 — let unit-cancellation check your work.
The SAT's favorite trap
Inverting the conversion (getting 0.011 instead of 88). Always write the units and watch them cancel.
The intuition

The mean is the balancing point — every value pulls on it, so one huge outlier drags it hard. The median is the middle value — outliers barely touch it. So: add or remove a very large value → mean moves a lot, median barely moves. The SAT asks this qualitatively ("what happens to the mean and median if…") more often than it asks you to compute.

Simple

Data: 4, 6, 8. Mean = 6, median = 6. Now add an outlier, 100 → 4, 6, 8, 100. What happens to the mean and the median?

Mean: 6 → 29.5 (bullied). Median: 6 → 7 (unfazed).
  1. New mean = 118/4 = 29.5 — dragged way up by 100.
  2. New median = middle of 4 numbers = (6 + 8)/2 = 7 — barely moved.
Mid

Data set {10, 11, 12, 13, 100} has mean 29.2 and median 12. If the outlier 100 is removed, what happens to each?

Mean: 29.2 → 11.5. Median: 12 → 11.5.
  1. Without the outlier: mean = 46/4 = 11.5 — dropped by 17.7.
  2. Median = (11 + 12)/2 = 11.5 — dropped by only 0.5. The median resisted.
Stretch

Ten students average 80 on a test. An 11th student scores 100. What's the new mean?

≈ 81.8
  1. Don't re-average from scratch — use totals: 10 × 80 = 800 points; +100 = 900; 900/11 ≈ 81.8.

Totals-first is the speed move for every "adding a value" question.

The can't-unsee-it
Outliers yank the mean, not the median — the median is the resistant one. For "new mean after adding" questions, work with totals, not the average.
The SAT's favorite trap
Answer choices that move the median as much as the mean. If the change is an extreme value, the median is the resistant one — that's the vocabulary the test uses.
The intuition

You will never compute a standard deviation on the SAT — you only need the idea: SD measures how spread out data is around the mean. Tight cluster → small SD; values scattered far and wide → large SD. Same mean, different SD = same center, different spread. Margin of error is the pollster's version: "52% ± 3%" means the truth is plausibly anywhere in 49–53%. Bigger sample → smaller margin of error → more precise.

Simple

Two datasets both have mean 50. A: {48, 49, 51, 52}. B: {10, 40, 60, 90}. Which has the greater standard deviation?

B
  1. B — its values sprawl far from 50; A's huddle near it. No calculation needed: spread, not center.
Mid

Team A scores {70, 70, 70, 70}; Team B scores {50, 60, 80, 90}. Compare their means and their standard deviations.

Equal means (70); B has the greater SD
  1. Both means are 70 — check: (50 + 60 + 80 + 90)/4 = 280/4 = 70. Same center.
  2. A has zero spread (SD = 0); B's values spread widely. Same mean, different spread — SD says nothing about the center.
Stretch

A poll of 1,000 voters finds 52% support for a candidate, margin of error ±3%. Which conclusion is valid: "The candidate definitely has majority support" or "Support is plausibly between 49% and 55%"?

"Support is plausibly between 49% and 55%"
  1. Plausible true support: 52% ± 3% → 49% to 55%.
  2. "Definitely has majority support" is invalid — 49% (below half) is inside the range. Margin of error defines a range of plausibility, not a guarantee.
The can't-unsee-it
SD = spread; compare visually, never compute. Margin of error = plausible range; larger samples shrink it.
The SAT's favorite trap
Treating "52% ± 3%" as "exactly 52%." Also: a bigger margin of error means less precision, not a different result.
The intuition

A two-way table is just sorted counting. Conditional probability ("given that…") means: throw away everyone who doesn't satisfy the condition, then compute the fraction within who's left. The #1 error is dividing by the grand total instead of the subgroup total.

Simple

60 students surveyed:

Students who play sports, by gender
Plays sportsDoesn'tTotal
Boys251540
Girls12820
Total372360
Given that a student plays sports, what's the probability they're a girl?

12/37
  1. Condition = "plays sports" → restrict to the 37 sports players. Girls among them: 12.
  2. P = 12/37 (not 12/60 — the condition shrank your world to 37 people).
Mid

200 voters surveyed: 120 support candidate X; of those 120, 80 are under 40. Of the 80 who don't support X, 50 are 40 or older. Given that a voter is under 40, what's the probability they support X?

8/11
  1. Build the under-40 group: 80 supporters under 40 + (80 − 50 = 30) non-supporters under 40 = 110 voters.
  2. P = 80/110 = 8/11. The denominator is the given group — 110, not 200.
Stretch

Using the table from the Simple example: (a) What fraction of girls play sports? (b) What fraction of sports players are girls? Why do they differ?

(a) 3/5   (b) 12/37 — because the denominators differ
  1. (a) Condition = girl → 12/20 = 3/5.
  2. (b) Condition = plays sports → 12/37.
  3. They differ because the denominator — the "given" group — changed. P(A given B) ≠ P(B given A). The test baits you with one when it asked for the other.
The can't-unsee-it
"Given X" → denominator = the X subgroup only. P(A|B) and P(B|A) are different questions with different denominators.
The SAT's favorite trap
The grand total. Every two-way table question includes a tempting choice with the total as denominator.
The intuition

On a scatterplot, the line of best fit captures the overall trend; individual points wobble around it. Its slope = predicted change in y per unit of x; its y-intercept = predicted y when x = 0. Predictions inside the data's range (interpolation) are trustworthy; predictions far outside it (extrapolation) are guesses dressed as math.

Simple

A line of best fit for (study hours, test score) is y = 2.5x + 60. Interpret the slope and intercept.

Slope: +2.5 points per hour. Intercept: 60 points at zero hours.
  1. Slope 2.5: each additional study hour predicts a 2.5-point score increase.
  2. Intercept 60: zero hours predicts a score of 60.
Mid

For (temperature °F, ice cream sales $), a line of best fit has slope 12 and y-intercept 40. (a) Interpret the slope. (b) Predict sales at 90°F. (c) The data covers 60–85°F — is the prediction trustworthy?

(a) $12 more sales per degree (b) $1,120 (c) Less — 90°F is outside the data
  1. (a) Each extra degree predicts $12 more in sales.
  2. (b) 12(90) + 40 = 1120 → $1,120.
  3. (c) 90°F is outside the observed 60–85°F → extrapolation → less trustworthy than an in-range prediction.
Stretch

The data covers 0–10 study hours. The line y = 2.5x + 60 predicts a score of 160 at 40 hours. Should you trust it?

No
  1. 40 hours is far outside the observed data (extrapolation).
  2. The linear trend may not continue — and a test maxes out at 100. The test rewards you for distrusting extrapolation.
The can't-unsee-it
Slope of the best-fit line = rate of predicted change. Trust interpolation; distrust extrapolation.
The SAT's favorite trap
Swapping the slope's meaning ("2.5 hours per point" instead of "2.5 points per hour"). Units: slope is always (y-units) per (x-unit).
4

Geometry & Trigonometry

≈15% of the test · Patterns 17–22 · Smallest domain — memorize the greatest hits

The intuition

The SAT reuses exactly two special right triangles, because their side ratios are fixed forever: 45-45-90 (isosceles): legs 1, 1 → hypotenuse √2; scale: leg L → hypotenuse L√2. 30-60-90: short leg 1, long leg √3, hypotenuse 2 — the hypotenuse is always double the short leg (the one across from 30°). Recognize them and you skip the Pythagorean theorem entirely.

Simple

A 45-45-90 triangle has legs of length 5. What is the hypotenuse?

5√2
  1. 45-45-90: hypotenuse = leg × √2 → 5√2.

5² + 5² = 50 = (5√2)² ✓

Mid

A 30-60-90 triangle has a hypotenuse of 14. Find the two legs.

Short leg 7, long leg 7√3
  1. Hypotenuse = 2 × short leg → short leg = 7.
  2. Long leg = √3 × short leg = 7√3.
Stretch

A square has diagonal 10. What is its area?

50
  1. The diagonal cuts the square into two 45-45-90 triangles: diagonal = side·√2 → side = 10/√2.
  2. Area = side² = (10/√2)² = 100/2 = 50. (No need to rationalize — squaring kills the root.)
The can't-unsee-it
45-45-90 → ×√2. 30-60-90 → 1 : √3 : 2, hypotenuse = 2 × short leg.
The SAT's favorite trap
In 30-60-90, mixing up which leg gets √3 — it's the long leg, across from the 60° angle. Draw it and label before computing.
The intuition

Similar triangles have identical angles — they're the same shape at different sizes, so every side is scaled by one common factor. Set up the proportion with corresponding sides (match them by the angles across from them), then cross-multiply. Shadows, mirrors, and "a line parallel to the base" are the classic setups.

Simple

Two similar triangles: the small one has sides 3, 4, 5. The side corresponding to 4 measures 12 in the large triangle. What's the scale factor, and the side corresponding to 3?

Scale factor 3; side is 9
  1. Scale = 12/4 = 3 → the side corresponding to 3 is 3 × 3 = 9.
Mid

In triangle ABC, segment DE connects the midpoints of sides AB and AC, with DE parallel to BC. If DE = 8, what is BC?

BC = 16
  1. The midpoints cut each side in half → triangle ADE is similar to ABC with linear scale 2.
  2. BC = 2 × DE = 2 × 8 = 16.
Stretch

A 6-ft person casts a 4-ft shadow. At the same time, a tree casts a 20-ft shadow. How tall is the tree?

30 ft
  1. Same sun angle → similar triangles: 6/4 = h/20 → h = (6 × 20)/4 = 30.
The can't-unsee-it
Similar = same angles → proportional sides. Match corresponding sides via their opposite angles, then cross-multiply.
The SAT's favorite trap
Proportioning non-corresponding sides (short-to-long). Label which side matches which before writing the proportion.
The intuition

The circle equation is just the Pythagorean theorem in disguise: every point (x, y) on the circle is distance r from the center (h, k). Burn in two things: the signs flip (x − 2 means h = +2), and the number on the right is r² — take the square root to get the radius.

Simple

(x − 2)² + (y + 3)² = 25. Find the center and radius.

Center (2, −3); radius 5
  1. Center: (2, −3) — the signs flip from what you see in the parentheses.
  2. Radius: √25 = 5 — the right side is r², not r.
Mid

Which of these points does NOT lie on the circle (x + 1)² + (y − 2)² = 13? A(2, 4), B(−3, 5), C(3, 5).

C(3, 5) is not on the circle
  1. A: (2+1)² + (4−2)² = 9 + 4 = 13 ✓ on the circle.
  2. B: (−3+1)² + (5−2)² = 4 + 9 = 13 ✓ on the circle.
  3. C: (3+1)² + (5−2)² = 16 + 9 = 25 ≠ 13 ✗ not on the circle.
Stretch

x² + y² − 6x + 8y = 0. Find the center and radius.

Center (3, −4); radius 5
  1. Complete the square: (x² − 6x + 9) + (y² + 8y + 16) = 9 + 16.
  2. → (x − 3)² + (y + 4)² = 25 → center (3, −4), radius 5.

What you add to the left, you must add to the right — that's the whole move.

The can't-unsee-it
Center (h, k) with flipped signs; radius = √(right side). Not in center form? Complete the square.
The SAT's favorite trap
Answering radius = 25, or center (−2, 3) — both wrong answers are always among the choices. Also: balancing both sides when completing the square.
The intuition

Sine = Opposite/Hypotenuse, Cosine = Adjacent/Hypotenuse, Tangent = Opposite/Adjacent — but "opposite" and "adjacent" depend on which acute angle you're standing at. Step one is always: point at the angle, label the three sides from its perspective. Bonus pattern: complementary angles swap sin and cos — sin(θ) = cos(90° − θ).

Simple

Right triangle, hypotenuse 10, one acute angle 30°. Find the side opposite the 30° angle.

5
  1. sin(30°) = opposite/10 → opposite = 10 × 0.5 = 5.

It's the short leg — matches the 30-60-90 ratio from Pattern 17 ✓

Mid

In right triangle DEF with right angle at E, angle D = 40°, and hypotenuse DF = 20. Find the length of side DE.

20·cos(40°) ≈ 15.3
  1. Plant yourself at D: DE is the adjacent side, DF the hypotenuse → use cosine.
  2. cos(40°) = DE/20 → DE = 20·cos(40°) ≈ 15.3 (calculator in degree mode).
Stretch

If sin(25°) ≈ 0.423, what is cos(65°)?

≈ 0.423
  1. 25° + 65° = 90° → complementary → cos(65°) = sin(25°) ≈ 0.423. No triangle needed.
The can't-unsee-it
Label opposite/adjacent from the given angle, then pick S, C, or T. Complementary angles: sin(θ) = cos(90° − θ).
The SAT's favorite trap
Using the wrong angle's perspective (labeling from angle A but plugging into angle B's ratio). Also: make sure the calculator is in degree mode.
The intuition

Bluebook gives you a reference sheet (area/circumference of a circle, triangle area, Pythagorean theorem, volumes of box/cylinder/sphere/cone/pyramid, etc.). Strategy: don't memorize what's given — memorize what's not (special triangle ratios, circle equation, SOHCAHTOA) and practice recognizing which formula a word problem wants. Composite shapes = add or subtract simple ones.

Simple

Cylinder with radius 3 and height 7. What's the volume?

63π
  1. V = πr²h = π(3)²(7) = 63π. (On the reference sheet — just reach for it.)
Mid

A rectangular box has dimensions 4 × 5 × 6. A cylinder has the same volume as the box and a radius of 5. What is the cylinder's height?

24/(5π) ≈ 1.53
  1. Box volume = 4 × 5 × 6 = 120.
  2. π(5)²h = 120 → h = 120/(25π) = 24/(5π) ≈ 1.53.
Stretch

A cone and a cylinder have the same radius and height. The cone's volume is 100. What's the cylinder's?

300
  1. Cone = ⅓(cylinder with same base and height) → cylinder = 3 × 100 = 300. The ⅓ is the whole question.
The can't-unsee-it
Use the reference sheet instead of memory for standard formulas; memorize only what's missing from it. Cone/pyramid = ⅓ of the corresponding prism.
The SAT's favorite trap
Diameter vs. radius (halve it first!), and forgetting the ⅓ on cones and pyramids — the single most common geometry distractor.
The intuition

For y = f(x): changes outside (f(x) + k) move the graph vertically and tell the truth (+3 = up 3). Changes inside (f(x − h)) move it horizontally and lie about direction (x − 2 = shift right 2; x + 4 = shift left 4). Think of it as: inside the parentheses, x has to work harder to get the same output, so the graph slides toward the sign's opposite.

Simple

f(x) = x². Describe the graphs of g(x) = f(x) + 3 and h(x) = f(x − 2).

g: up 3 · h: right 2
  1. g(x) = f(x) + 3: outside → vertical, truthful → shift up 3.
  2. h(x) = f(x − 2): inside → horizontal, lies → shift right 2.
Mid

The graph of f(x) = |x| is shifted 3 units right and 1 unit up. Write the new equation.

g(x) = |x − 3| + 1
  1. Right 3 → inside, opposite sign: |x − 3|.
  2. Up 1 → outside, truthful: + 1 → g(x) = |x − 3| + 1.
Stretch

The graph of y = f(x) is shifted 4 units left and 2 units down. Which equation describes the new graph?

y = f(x + 4) − 2
  1. Left 4 → inside, opposite sign: f(x + 4).
  2. Down 2 → outside, truthful: − 2 → y = f(x + 4) − 2.
The can't-unsee-it
Outside = vertical, truthful. Inside = horizontal, reversed.
The SAT's favorite trap
"Shift left 4" → writing f(x − 4). The inside shift always goes the "wrong" way — that's exactly what the question is testing.

Test-day strategy

Content is the weapon; these are the rules of engagement.

1

Module 1 is the whole game

Your overall Module 1 performance routes you to a harder or easier Module 2 — position within the module doesn't change a question's weight. So prioritize accuracy over speed across the entire first module; careless misses there are the surest way to lose your preferred route. Accuracy across the whole module, first.

2

Never leave a blank

No guessing penalty: an eliminated guess beats a blank 100% of the time. With ~2 minutes left, fill every remaining question. Blank = wrong, always.

3

Desmos is a power tool, not a lifestyle

It trivializes: systems (graph both, click the intersection), quadratics (read roots/vertex), algebra verification, tables, means, regression. It wastes time on: simple percents, one-step algebra, anything solvable mentally in ~10 seconds. Desmos for graphs, systems, and verification; brain for arithmetic.

4

Pace like a banker

≈95 seconds per question — but not evenly. Easy ones should take 30–45s, buying 2–3 minutes for the hard ones at each module's end. If a question eats 3 minutes with no progress, flag it, move on, come back — stuck time is the most expensive kind.

5

Word problems: translate before you calculate

≈30% of questions are "in context." Write the equation first — define the variable! — then solve. Most word-problem errors are translation errors, not math errors.

6

Grid-ins have rules

Negative answers are allowed — enter the minus sign. Fractions and decimals are both accepted (no mixed numbers — enter 7/2, not 3½). If the value won't fit, enter the decimal, truncating or rounding at the fourth digit; never include %, commas, or $.

7

Use the reference sheet

It's in the app — area, volume, Pythagorean theorem, and more. Don't burn memory on what's given; burn it on what's not: exponents (Pattern 8), special triangles (17), the circle equation (19), SOHCAHTOA (20).

8

The "does this make sense" filter

A percent increase over 100% should more than double; a probability over 1 is impossible; a slope described backwards (hours per point) is a trap. One second of sanity-checking catches a third of careless errors.

The 900 → 1400 study order

Maximum points per hour. Read a domain here, then drill it on Khan Academy or Bluebook before moving on.

1

Algebra · Patterns 1–4

≈35% of the test; the foundation everything else stands on. Master first.

2

Advanced Math core · Patterns 5–9

≈35% more; quadratics + exponents + equivalent expressions are the densest point-per-hour topics on the test.

3

Problem-Solving & Data · Patterns 11–16

≈15%; mostly reading comprehension plus two formulas. Fast points.

4

Geometry & Trig · Patterns 17–22

≈15%; memorize the five greatest hits — special triangles, circle equation, SOHCAHTOA, ⅓ volumes, transformations — and move on.

5

Timed practice tests + drill your misses

Bluebook practice tests + Khan Academy drills. Review every miss by asking: "Which pattern did I fail to see?"
