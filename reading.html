SAT Reading Crash Course

The reading section is a pattern test wearing a content costume. The passages change; the question types and the traps don't. This course distills the reading-focused question types into nine patterns — each with the intuition in plain words, one simple example, and one stretch example. Learn them until you can't unsee them, then do your reps on Khan Academy and Bluebook.

Figures above describe the full Reading & Writing section; this guide covers its reading half — roughly 24–29 of the 54 questions.

How to use this guide
  1. Attempt before you reveal. On every practice question, tap an answer before you read the breakdown. Getting caught by a trap is how you learn to see it.
  2. Name the trap out loud. Each breakdown labels why every wrong choice is wrong. Steal that language until it's automatic — that's the real skill.
  3. Go in order. Pattern 1 (the wrong-answer taxonomy) is the operating system everything else runs on. Master it first.

The battlefield

How the section works

  • 54 questions, 64 minutes, split into two 32-minute modules — about 1 minute 11 seconds per question.
  • Every question gets its own short passage (25–150 words) about literature, history, social studies, humanities, or science. One passage, one question — no long reading sets.
  • Questions that test similar skills are grouped together, arranged roughly easiest to hardest within each group. Early questions are your on-ramp — don't skip them.
  • Adaptive: your Module 1 performance routes you to an easier or harder Module 2. Harder Module 2 means access to the top score bands.
  • No penalty for wrong answers. A guess is worth 25%; a blank is worth 0%. Answer everything.

The domains, in the order they appear on test day:

Craft & Structure→ Information & Ideas→ Standard English Conventions→ Expression of Ideas

This guide covers the two highlighted reading domains (roughly 24–29 of the 54 questions, per College Board's published specs). The grammar and expression-of-ideas questions belong to the companion writing material.

Pattern 1 · The operating system

1

The Wrong-Answer Taxonomy

The SAT never writes a wrong answer by accident. Every wrong choice is a trap built to catch one specific mistake a rushed reader makes — and there are only about four traps, showing up on nearly every question. The moment you stop asking "which one feels right?" and start asking "how is this one wrong?", the whole section changes. Correct answers survive scrutiny; wrong ones always have one cracked word if you poke them.

The move: learn the four traps, then interrogate every choice

  1. True but doesn't answer the question asked. Accurate to the passage, wrong for the question. (The most common trap on the test.)
  2. Half-right, half-wrong. First half matches the text; second half twists, adds, or reverses something.
  3. Extreme language. "Always," "never," "completely," "all," "proves" — when the passage said "sometimes," "may," "suggests." A single quantifier word flips a true claim into a false one.
  4. Not in the text / beyond the text. Plausible, even real-world true — but the passage never says it. Your outside knowledge is not evidence.

The master discipline: when two choices look alike, find the one word that differs — that word is the trap. And if you can't point to textual proof for every word of your chosen answer, you don't have the answer yet.

Drill 1 — Name the trap

Warm-up

Passage

A 2024 study of 200 college students found that those who slept at least 7 hours scored slightly higher on a memory quiz than those who slept less.

Choice: "Sleep completely determines memory performance." Which trap is it?

A
Wrong trap

The choice isn't merely beside the point — it overstates the finding.

B
Correct — extreme language

"Completely determines" vs. "slightly higher." One quantifier flips true into false.

C
Wrong trap

No true half anchors this claim; the whole thing is overstated.

D
Wrong trap

The topic is in the text — the failure is the extreme word, not missing evidence.

Drill 2 — Name the trap

Warm-up

Passage

Dr. Amara Okafor tested whether background music affects reading comprehension. Participants read one passage in silence and one with instrumental music. Scores were lower with music — but only among participants who found the music distracting.

Question: "Which choice best states the study's main finding?" Choice: "Okafor's experiment involved two reading conditions." Which trap is it?

A
Correct — true but off-question

Accurate detail (two conditions), wrong for what was asked (the main finding).

B
Wrong trap

No extreme quantifiers; the failing is relevance, not intensity.

C
Wrong trap

Fully true, not half. The choice answers a question nobody asked.

D
Wrong trap

The evidence is in the passage; the problem is what the question asked.

Warm-up

A
Wrong trap

The passage never supports this, so it can't be the "true but irrelevant" case.

Wrong trap

No extreme words — the failing is unsupported evidence, not intensity.

Wrong trap

Plausible, maybe even real-world true — but the passage never says it. No evidence = wrong.

Drill 4 — Name the trap

Warm-up

Passage

Dr. Liu's experiment showed that plants grew taller with more light — but growth leveled off past 12 hours of light per day.

Choice: "Plants grew taller with more light, so greenhouses should run lights 24 hours a day." Which trap is it?

A
Wrong trap

It's on-topic; the issue is the second half contradicts the passage.

B
Wrong trap

Not an intensity problem — a true claim followed by a false recommendation.

C
Correct — half-right, half-wrong

True half: more light grew taller plants. Wrong half: 24-hour lighting helps — growth plateaus past 12 hours.

D
Wrong trap

The first half is explicitly in the text, so this isn't the beyond-text pattern.

Practice A — the taxonomy in action

Simple

Passage

Marine biologist Elena Ruiz spent six summers tracking humpback whales off the coast of Baja California. She found that individual whales returned to the same feeding grounds year after year, suggesting strong site loyalty. She also noted that whales occasionally altered their routes in response to shifting krill populations, but she did not observe any permanent abandonment of a feeding site.

Based on the text, what did Ruiz find about humpback whales' feeding behavior?

A
Wrong — half-right, half-wrong

Route adjustments are real — but the passage explicitly denies permanent abandonment.

B
Correct

"Loyalty to specific feeding grounds" + "temporarily adjust their routes" — every word is supported.

C
Wrong — extreme language

"Never … under any circumstances" vs. "occasionally altered." Dead on one word.

D
Wrong — beyond the text

The "why" of Baja and the "largest krill populations" are never mentioned. No evidence = wrong.

Practice B — the 650→750 separator

Stretch

Passage

The city of Lisbon's "green corridor" project planted 20,000 trees along former industrial land. A 2024 study found that neighborhoods adjacent to the corridor experienced summer temperatures 2–3°C lower than comparable neighborhoods without tree cover. However, the study's authors cautioned that the cooling effect varied significantly with tree density and that the results could not be generalized to cities with different climates.

Which statement about the Lisbon study is most accurate?

A
Wrong — extreme + beyond text

"Proved" and "all cities" are directly contradicted: the authors refused to generalize.

B
Correct

"Found a cooling effect" (2–3°C lower) + "warned against applying findings too broadly" (could not be generalized). Both halves hold.

C
Wrong — true-but-irrelevant

The comparison it describes (industrial vs. residential) isn't what the study tested.

D
Wrong — half-right, half-wrong

Tree density mattered — but "the only factor" is an extreme claim the text never makes. This is the trap that separates 650s from 750s: the first half is so true you stop reading.

One-line takeaway

Wrong answers are built from four traps — poke every word until each choice proves itself against the text.

The trap

When two choices look alike, the one word that differs is the trap. If your pick has a word you can't prove from the text, you don't have the answer yet.

Pattern 2 · Information & Ideas

2

Main Idea & Purpose: The Bucket Test

The correct main-idea answer is a summary of the whole passage, not a detail from one sentence. Think of it as a bucket: it must be big enough to hold everything the passage says, but not so big it holds things the passage doesn't. Wrong answers fail the bucket test in exactly two ways — too narrow (true, but only covers one sentence) or too broad (covers the passage plus stuff it never claimed).

The bucket test in one glance: the dashed outline is the passage itself. The correct answer is the bucket that exactly fits it — no smaller, no bigger.

The move: summarize before you look

  1. Read the passage, look away, and say "this passage is about ___" in one sentence. That's your prediction — the correct choice will cover the same ground.
  2. "Best states the main idea" and "best describes the overall structure" are the same question in two coats: both want the whole passage summarized, not a highlight reel.

Practice A

Simple

Passage

Honeybees communicate the location of food sources through the "waggle dance," a figure-eight movement that encodes distance and direction. Recent research shows that bees also perform a shorter "tremble dance" when a food source is overcrowded, signaling to other foragers to wait. Together, these dances form a surprisingly sophisticated system for managing the colony's foraging efforts.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A
Wrong — too narrow

True from a single sentence. The bucket must also hold the tremble dance.

B
Correct

"Multiple dances" + "coordinate the colony's foraging" — the passage's full arc in one sentence.

C
Wrong — too broad

All insects over millions of years? The passage never makes that claim.

D
Wrong — too narrow

One-sentence detail. Tempting because it's true — wrong because it's small.

Practice B

Stretch

Passage

In the 1920s, urban planners championed the "garden city" movement, arguing that integrating green space into cities would improve public health. By the 1960s, however, many garden-city developments had been absorbed into sprawling suburbs, and critics declared the movement a failure. Yet recent scholarship suggests a more complicated verdict: while the movement's utopian ambitions were never realized, its emphasis on green space quietly shaped modern zoning codes and park planning.

Which choice best describes the overall structure and main point of the text?

A
Correct

Rise → apparent decline → partial vindication mirrors the passage's three sentences.

B
Wrong — opposite

"Unqualified success" reverses the passage's measured verdict.

C
Wrong — too narrow

Final-sentence detail only. The bucket needs the full rise-and-fall arc.

D
Wrong — too narrow

First-sentence content. Also: notice C and D are both too-narrow — the test stacks them.

One-line takeaway

The right answer is the bucket that fits — big enough for the whole passage, no bigger.

The trap

A detail from a single sentence dressed up as the main idea (too narrow); a grand claim the passage never made (too broad).

Pattern 3 · Information & Ideas

3

Detail Retrieval — "According to the Text"

These are the easiest points on the test — and students still miss them by answering from memory instead of from the text. The pattern: the question asks something specific, the passage states it plainly, and the traps paraphrase it almost right. Treat it like a lookup task — go find the exact sentence, then match the claim word by word.

The move: the lookup discipline

  1. Spot the trigger: "According to the text" / "Based on the text, what is true…" means the answer is stated, not inferred.
  2. Find the sentence that addresses it and read it word by word — no skimming.
  3. Check the choice with the same precision: every claim in the choice must land in the passage. One unspoken word is enough to kill it.

Practice A

Simple

Passage

The axolotl, a salamander native to Lake Xochimilco in Mexico, can regenerate lost limbs, portions of its heart, and even parts of its brain. Unlike most amphibians, it retains its larval features throughout its adult life, a phenomenon known as neoteny. Wild axolotl populations have declined sharply due to habitat loss and pollution, and the species is now critically endangered.

According to the text, what is true of axolotls?

A
Wrong — beyond the text

"Only" is never stated. The regeneration claim is true; the exclusivity is invented.

B
Correct

Both halves stated plainly. The lookup finds the sentence; the sentence confirms the choice.

C
Wrong — not in the text

Migration is never mentioned. No sentence, no points.

D
Wrong — opposite

"Declined sharply" ≠ "fully recovered." The detail-memory trap in pure form.

Practice B

Stretch

Passage

Dr. Amara Okafor's 2023 experiment tested whether background music affects reading comprehension. Participants read two passages — one in silence, one with instrumental music — and answered questions afterward. Scores were 8% lower with music, but only among participants who reported finding the music distracting; those who said they barely noticed the music performed equally well in both conditions.

Based on the text, which finding would Okafor's results most strongly support?

A
Wrong — extreme

"Always" vs. "only among" — the passage's own qualifier is the weapon.

B
Correct

The finding is the conditional: it depends on perceived distraction.

C
Wrong — beyond the text

No comparison of music types was ever made.

D
Wrong — overgeneralization

"Best," "all students," "strategy" — three unspoken claims wrapped in one sentence.

One-line takeaway

Look it up in the text; match the claim word by word; never answer from memory.

The trap

Choices that add one unspoken word ("only," "always," "all") to an otherwise accurate restatement.

Pattern 4 · Information & Ideas

4

Inference: The One Safe Step

"Which choice most logically completes the text?" looks scary but follows a strict rule: the correct answer is one small logical step beyond the passage — never two. If the passage hands you A and the answer requires A→B→C, it's too far. The right answer is the thing the passage is pointing at but hasn't quite said. Ask yourself: "What would the author agree with, given only what's written?"

The whole pattern: the correct inference is the stone you can reach in a single step from the evidence. Anything requiring a second step — a new fact, a judgment, a policy opinion — is a trap wearing a smart conclusion's clothes.

The move: count the steps

  1. Link each piece of the answer back to a sentence in the passage. If every link exists, it's one step.
  2. If you need to supply a fact, a judgment, or a policy opinion the passage never hints at — you're on step two. Kill it.

Practice A

Simple

Passage

Sea otters must eat roughly 25% of their body weight daily to survive in cold Pacific waters. Unlike seals and whales, they lack a thick layer of blubber, so they rely on their exceptionally dense fur — the thickest of any mammal — to trap insulating air against their skin. When oil spills coat their fur, the air layer collapses.

Which choice most logically completes the text?

A
Correct — one step

Insulation depends on fur; oil kills the fur's insulation. Conclusion: vulnerability. One step.

B
Wrong — two steps

A "should" is a policy judgment the passage never hints at.

C
Wrong — extreme + beyond text

"Never" is doing all the killing here.

D
Wrong — beyond the text

No cross-species comparison of total intake is given.

Practice B

Stretch

Passage

In a 2022 study, economists compared two towns with similar demographics. Town A introduced a congestion tax on downtown driving; Town B did not. Over three years, downtown retail revenue in Town A fell 12%, while in Town B it rose 4%. However, air quality monitors in Town A recorded a 20% drop in particulate pollution, and pedestrian traffic in its downtown increased 30%. Town B showed no significant change in either measure.

Which choice most logically completes the text?

A
Wrong — two steps too far

One comparison can't crown the "best policy for all cities." Feels smart; leaps anyway.

B
Correct — one step

Down costs + up gains = trade-offs. Nothing added.

C
Wrong — beyond the text

Store quality is invented; the tax is the only stated difference.

D
Wrong — reversed logic

Pollution fell while revenue fell — it cannot explain the revenue drop.

One-line takeaway

The answer is one logical step past the text — if it needs two steps or a leap, it's wrong.

The trap

Answers that feel like smart conclusions but require information or judgments the passage never provides.

Pattern 5 · Craft & Structure

5

Command of Evidence: Text & Data

Two flavors, one rule. In textual evidence questions, you're given a claim and asked which quote best supports or illustrates it — so match the specific claim, not the general topic. In quantitative questions, a passage is paired with a table or graph, and the correct answer is the only choice true of BOTH the text and the data. Students fail these by checking only one of the two. The move you can't unsee: for every choice, ask "does the text say this AND does the chart show this?"

True of the chart but not the passage? Dead. True of the passage but not the chart? Dead. The answer must live in the overlap — check both, every time.

The move: match the claim, check both sources

  1. Textual flavor ("most effectively illustrates her claim"): match the exact claim, not the general topic. The choice that matches the topic while ignoring the claim is Trap 1.
  2. Quantitative flavor: before reading the choices, note the chart's units and what each row and column actually measures.
  3. For every choice, run the double check: text AND chart. One "no" kills the choice.

Practice A — textual evidence

Simple

Passage

Coral reefs are often called the "rainforests of the sea" for their biodiversity. Marine ecologist Dr. Priya Nair argues that this nickname is misleading, however. In a recent paper, she contends that the metaphor encourages people to think of reefs as pristine wildernesses untouched by humans, when in fact many reefs have been shaped by centuries of human fishing practices.

Which quotation from Nair's paper would most effectively illustrate her claim about the "rainforests of the sea" nickname?

A
Wrong — true-but-irrelevant

Supports the nickname, not her criticism of it. Trap 1 in formal dress.

B
Correct

Illustrates exactly her claim: nickname → vision of untouched wilderness → obscures human history.

C
Wrong — off-claim

A comparison she never makes; doesn't touch her argument.

D
Wrong — half-right, half-wrong

"Shaped by fishing" ≠ "unchanged fishing." The second half twists her words.

Practice B — quantitative evidence

Stretch

Passage

Researchers hypothesized that a new tutoring program would improve math scores more for students who attended at least 80% of sessions than for those who attended fewer. The table shows average score gains (in points) for participants grouped by attendance.

Table: average math score gain by tutoring attendance.
AttendanceAvg. score gain
90–100% of sessions+18
80–89% of sessions+15
60–79% of sessions+6
Below 60%+2

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the researchers' hypothesis?

A
Wrong — half-right (overreach)

True of the data, but misses the 80% threshold precision the hypothesis demands.

B
Wrong — beyond the data

"Proving any attendance is sufficient" isn't in the data and ignores the hypothesis.

C
Wrong — wrong side

The question asks you to support the hypothesis; this choice attacks it.

D
Correct

Chart ✓ ("at least 15" for both ≥80% groups) and passage ✓ (the hypothesis). Overlap = answer.

One-line takeaway

For data questions, the answer must be true of the chart AND the passage — check both, every time.

The trap

Choices supported by the chart but contradicting the passage's claim (or vice versa); choices that describe the data without addressing the hypothesis.

Pattern 6 · Craft & Structure

6

Words in Context: Let the Sentence Hand You the Meaning

The SAT doesn't test whether you know a word's dictionary definition — it tests whether you can let the sentence hand you the meaning. The sentence always contains a logic clue. Cover the word with your thumb, read the logic around it, predict a plain synonym, then match. The trap answers are the word's other common meanings — the ones your brain reaches for first.

Contrastbut, however, yet, rather than Causebecause, so, leading to Restatementa comma phrase saying it twice Examplefor example, such as

The move: the thumb trick

  1. Cover the word. Read the sentence around it and find the logic clue — contrast? cause? restatement? example?
  2. Predict a plain-English stand-in ("decreased," "fake," "careful").
  3. Uncover and match. If two choices are close, check the tone: is the word praise or criticism right here?

Practice A

Simple

Passage

The mayor's proposal to build a downtown stadium was initially popular, but support waned as cost estimates doubled and then tripled over the course of a year.

As used in the text, "waned" most nearly means

A
Wrong — opposite

"But" rules it out: support went the other way from "popular."

B
Correct

Contrast clue ("but") + rising costs → support diminished. The thumb trick works.

C
Wrong — too vague

"Shifted" is directionless; the sentence demands a direction.

D
Wrong — extreme

Faded ≠ vanished. Trap 3 again — extreme words are everywhere on this test.

Practice B

Stretch

Passage

Critics called the novel's plot contrived, arguing that the sudden inheritance, the long-lost twin, and the perfectly timed storm felt less like organic developments than machinery assembled to rescue the protagonist.

As used in the text, "contrived" most nearly means

A
Wrong — the other meaning

"Clever" is a valid sense of "contrived" — just not the one this sentence is using.

B
Correct

Organic vs. machinery = real vs. fake. The tone is negative; the meaning follows.

C
Wrong — near-miss

Complex ≠ artificial. The contrast is about naturalness, not intricacy.

D
Wrong — wrong tone

"Deliberate" is neutral. The critics are criticizing.

One-line takeaway

Don't define the word — let the sentence's logic (contrast, cause, restatement) hand you the meaning.

The trap

The word's other familiar meaning; a near-synonym with the wrong tone (positive vs. negative).

Pattern 7 · Craft & Structure

7

Text Structure & Function: Name the Move

These questions ask what a sentence or the passage does, not what it says. Strip the content, watch the move: does it introduce, give an example, present a counterargument, concede a point, draw a conclusion? Authors make only a handful of moves. Learn to name the move — "that's an example," "that's a qualification" — and the answer becomes obvious.

The move: the move vocabulary

  1. Introduce / define / summarize — sets up or wraps up.
  2. Illustrate — gives an example of a claim.
  3. Concede (admits a point) or qualify (adds a limit).
  4. Counter (pushes back) or conclude (lands the argument).

Practice A

Simple

Passage

Octopuses are renowned problem-solvers. In one experiment, an octopus learned to unscrew a jar lid to reach a crab inside — after watching another octopus do it just once. Such rapid observational learning was once thought to be limited to vertebrates like primates and crows.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence?

A
Wrong — wrong move

No contrasting viewpoint appears — just a prior belief the finding overturns.

B
Wrong — wrong move

Motivation is never discussed. Ignore content you want to see; name what's there.

C
Correct

"Once thought limited to…" is the tell: the sentence is here to make the finding feel significant.

D
Wrong — wrong move

The result was already stated. This sentence comments on its importance.

Practice B

Stretch

Passage

Some historians argue that the printing press caused the Reformation by spreading Luther's ideas. Yet Luther's theses spread rapidly through Germany in 1517 largely via handwritten copies and word of mouth, months before printed editions appeared in most cities.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence?

A
Correct

"Yet" is the tell. Qualifies = complicates without destroying. That's the move, named precisely.

B
Wrong — wrong move

Response ≠ introduction. Watch for direction, not just topic.

C
Wrong — one degree too strong

"No role" over-presses what the sentence does. The classic verb trap.

D
Wrong — wrong direction

It pushes back on the consensus, not gathers it up.

One-line takeaway

Ignore what the sentence says; name what it does — example, concession, qualification, conclusion.

The trap

Verbs that are one degree too strong ("refutes" vs. "qualifies," "proves" vs. "suggests").

Pattern 8 · Craft & Structure

8

Cross-Text Connections: Agree, Disagree, or Qualify

Two short texts, one question: how do they relate? There are only three relationships that matter: agree (Text 2 supports Text 1), disagree (Text 2 challenges Text 1), or qualify (Text 2 partly agrees but adds a limit). The move: summarize each text's stance in five words, then compare stances. Never compare details — compare positions.

Three relationships, three pictures. "Qualify" — one arrow falling short of the other — is the one that shows up on the hardest questions.

The move: the stance habit

  1. Cover Text 2. Summarize Text 1's position in five words ("beekeeping good for cities").
  2. Do the same for Text 2 ("beekeeping harms native bees").
  3. Compare positions, never details: agree, disagree, or qualify?

Practice A

Simple

Text 1

Urban beekeeping has exploded in popularity, with rooftop hives appearing in cities worldwide. Proponents argue that city bees boost local pollination and reconnect urban residents with nature.

Text 2

Entomologist Dr. Luis Vega warns that the urban beekeeping boom may harm native pollinators. Honeybees, he notes, outcompete native wild bees for limited floral resources, and most city hives are kept by hobbyists with little training in disease management.

How would Vega (Text 2) most likely respond to the proponents described in Text 1?

A
Wrong — opposite

Stances: pro (T1) vs. con (T2). Agreement isn't on the table.

B
Correct

Disagreement, stated as a trade-off: harms outweigh claimed benefits. Positions compared, not details.

C
Wrong — extreme

"Banned in all cities" — his warning never becomes a demand.

D
Wrong — beyond the text

Survival is never questioned; effects on native pollinators are the topic.

Practice B

Stretch

Text 1

Psychologist Carol Dweck's "growth mindset" research suggests that students who believe abilities can be developed achieve more than those who believe abilities are fixed. Schools worldwide have adopted mindset interventions based on this work.

Text 2

A 2023 meta-analysis found that growth-mindset interventions produce small but statistically significant improvements in academic achievement, particularly for at-risk students. The authors conclude that mindset programs are a useful tool, though not the transformative solution some advocates have claimed.

Based on the texts, how would the authors of Text 2 most likely characterize the claim in Text 1 that mindset beliefs affect achievement?

A
Wrong — too strong

They found significant improvements; "without merit" is total rejection.

B
Correct

"Useful tool, though not transformative" = qualification. Partial agreement with a ceiling.

C
Wrong — too strong

Their own data shows real effects; "contradicted" misreads it.

D
Wrong — one-word trap

"Particularly" → "only." This single-word twist shows up constantly at high difficulty.

One-line takeaway

Summarize each text's stance in five words, then name the relationship: agree, disagree, or qualify.

The trap

"Particularly" twisted into "only"; partial agreement presented as total rejection (or vice versa).

Pattern 9 · The meta-pattern

9

Reading Strategy: 71 Seconds a Question

With roughly 71 seconds per question and one question per passage, the winning strategy is mechanical, not heroic: read the question stem first (it tells you what to hunt for), read the passage with that mission, predict the answer in your own words before looking at the choices, then eliminate with named reasons. Students who "pick what feels right" are donating points.

  1. Question stem first. "Main idea?" → read for the overall point. "According to the text?" → hunt the detail. "Most nearly means?" → find the logic clue. The stem tells you what to hunt for.
  2. Read actively, annotate lightly. One underline: the sentence carrying the main claim. In Bluebook, use the annotation tool — mark the claim, not everything.
  3. Predict before you peek. Cover the choices; say the answer in plain words. This inoculates you against trap phrasing.
  4. Eliminate with a reason. For each choice, name it: "too narrow," "extreme," "beyond text," "half-right." If you can't name why it's wrong, you haven't earned the elimination.
  5. Keep moving. 71 seconds is the budget. If a question eats 2 minutes, it's stealing from two easier questions later. Flag it, move on, return if time remains.
  6. Answer everything. No penalty for wrong answers. A guess among four choices is worth 25% — a blank is worth 0%.

Two missions in action

Stem: "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?"

Mission: hunt the overall point (Pattern 2), not a detail. Predict before peeking: after reading, say "this passage is about ___" in one sentence, then match.

Stem: "Which choice most logically completes the text?" — with a data table.

Mission: one safe step (Pattern 4) that is true of both text and data (Pattern 5). Predict: "the answer will say [the trend] but won't claim [more than the data shows]." Then eliminate anything needing two steps.

01:11

Train the pace. Drill a single question against 1:11 until the elimination discipline survives the clock — then string questions into a 32-minute module run.

One-line takeaway

Stem first, predict before peeking, eliminate with named reasons, never leave a blank.

The trap

Spending 3 minutes on one hard question and rushing three easy ones — the test rewards steady pace, not heroics.

Bonus · The algorithm

Adaptive Module Strategy

The digital SAT adapts: Module 1 is a mix of difficulties; your performance routes you to an easier or harder Module 2. A harder Module 2 means access to the top score bands. Within each module, work the questions in order — each domain group runs easy→hard, and the early questions are your on-ramp.

  1. Module 1 is the gateway. Take it seriously from question 1 — a strong Module 1 unlocks the harder, higher-ceiling Module 2. Don't "warm up" into the test.
  2. Every question counts; none can be skipped strategically. There is no penalty for wrong answers, so never leave a blank — guess on anything you're stuck on.
  3. Budget ~71 seconds per question. That's 32 minutes for 27 questions per module. If one question passes 2 minutes, flag it and move — easy questions later are worth just as much.
  4. Use the tools. Bluebook's highlight and annotation tools are free, and so is the built-in calculator for data questions. Cross out eliminated choices with the strikethrough tool — it makes Pattern 1's elimination discipline visual.
  5. Don't get psyched out by difficulty. If Module 2 feels harder, that's good news — it means Module 1 went well. Stay steady; the questions are worth more.
  6. Trust the section's order. Craft & Structure → Information & Ideas → grammar → Expression of Ideas. If grammar is your strength, bank those points fast and buy time for the harder reading questions.
One-line takeaway

Crush Module 1 to unlock the hard Module 2, keep a 71-second pace, and never leave a question blank.

Remember

The only guaranteed zero on this test is a blank. The algorithm can't reward what you never answer.

Review

Cheat Sheet: All Nine Patterns

One line each. Re-read this page the morning of the test — then go predict, eliminate, and keep moving.

  1. R1
    The Wrong-Answer Taxonomy. Ask "how is this one wrong?" — then name it: true-but-off-question, half-right, extreme, beyond text.
  2. R2
    Main Idea & Purpose. The bucket must fit: big enough for the whole passage, no bigger.
  3. R3
    Detail Retrieval. Look it up in the text; match the claim word by word; never answer from memory.
  4. R4
    Inference. One small step past the text. Two steps = wrong.
  5. R5
    Command of Evidence. Textual: match the exact claim. Data: true of the chart AND the passage — check both.
  6. R6
    Words in Context. Thumb over the word; let the sentence's logic (contrast, cause, restatement) hand you the meaning.
  7. R7
    Structure & Function. Ignore what it says; name what it does. Watch for one-degree-too-strong verbs.
  8. R8
    Cross-Text Connections. Stance each text in five words; name the relationship: agree, disagree, or qualify.
  9. R9
    Strategy. Stem first, predict before peeking, eliminate with a named reason, 71-second pace, never blank.
  10. ★
    Adaptive. Crush Module 1 to unlock the hard Module 2.
