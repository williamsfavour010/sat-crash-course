Before you start

Know the terrain

Writing questions live inside the Reading & Writing section: two 32-minute modules, 27 questions each. Grammar comes on test day in a fixed order — the section groups similar skills together, arranged roughly easiest to hardest within each group.

54 questions

across 64 minutes; ~71 seconds a question. Keep moving — flagged questions can wait.

≈11–15 grammar Qs

Standard English Conventions (~26% of the section) + Expression of Ideas (~8–12 ≈ 20%) — weights per College Board's published specs.

0 penalty

No penalty for wrong answers. Never leave a question blank — guess on anything stuck.

Domain order on test day (your half is the last two):

Craft & Structure→ Information & Ideas→ Standard English Conventions→ Expression of Ideas
  • Every question carries its own short passage (25–150 words) — you never carry context far.
  • The section adapts: a strong Module 1 unlocks a harder Module 2 — and a harder Module 2 unlocks the top score bands.
  • Within each module, work in order: early questions in a group are the easy on-ramp to the hard ones.
Part A · Modules W1–W8

Standard English Conventions

The grammar loop. This is the SAT's most mechanical domain: a small rule set, applied the same way every test. Modules W1–W6 are the non-negotiables — nail those and nearly every grammar question is readable.

W1

Sentence boundaries

Standard English Conventions
Must-know

The intuition

The SAT's favorite sentence crime is the comma splice: two complete sentences glued with just a comma. The fix menu is tiny — a period, a semicolon, or a comma + FANBOYS conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so).

Also watch fragments (no main verb — "Running down the street") and run-ons (two sentences with no punctuation at all). The can't-unsee-it test: cover each side of the punctuation — if both sides could stand alone as sentences, a bare comma between them is always wrong.

Simple

The museum extended its hours for the new exhibit, ___ drew record crowds.

  1. ANO CHANGE — comma
  2. Bexhibit; it
  3. Cexhibit, it
  4. Dexhibit it
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

Both sides are complete sentences ("The museum extended its hours for the new exhibit" / "it drew record crowds"). A bare comma between two complete sentences = comma splice. B uses a semicolon — period-strength.

A and C are comma splices. D is a run-on.

Stretch

Having trained for months, the marathon runner felt confident; ___ she had never raced in such extreme heat before.

  1. ANO CHANGE — semicolon
  2. Bconfident, although
  3. Cconfident although
  4. Dconfident: although
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

"Although she had never raced in such extreme heat before" is a dependent clause — it can't stand alone. A semicolon needs complete sentences on both sides, so A is wrong. B properly attaches the dependent clause with a comma + subordinating conjunction.

C is a run-on-ish attachment with no punctuation. D misuses the colon: a colon must follow a complete thought, and "although" makes what follows dependent, breaking the colon's announcing structure.

One-line takeaway

Two complete sentences need period-strength punctuation — period, semicolon, or comma + FANBOYS. A bare comma is never enough.

The trap

A semicolon where one side is a fragment; a colon that doesn't follow a complete thought.

W2

Punctuation logic — what each mark signals

Standard English Conventions
Must-know

The intuition

Punctuation marks are traffic signals, and each has exactly one job. The move: ask "are both sides complete sentences?" — if yes, you're in semicolon/period territory. Ask "does the second half explain the first?" — that's colon/dash territory.

,
Comma — brief pause

Separates items, sets off nonessential info, follows introductions. Never joins two complete sentences alone.

;
Semicolon — full stop in disguise

Complete sentence on both sides, always. Think of it as a period wearing a costume.

:
Colon — drumroll

Must follow a complete sentence, and what comes after explains, lists, or elaborates.

—
Single dash — dramatic colon

Same rules as a colon: a complete sentence must come before it.

– –
Pair of dashes or commas — parentheses

Fences around nonessential info. They must match: open with a dash, close with a dash.

∅
No punctuation

The default. When the sentence flows without a break, don't insert a mark just because it's an SAT question.

Both sides complete sentences? → semicolon or period territory
Second half explains the first? → colon or dash territory

Simple

The scientist's most famous discovery ___ penicillin ___ revolutionized medicine.

  1. ANO CHANGE — no punctuation
  2. Bdiscovery: penicillin,
  3. Cdiscovery—penicillin—
  4. Ddiscovery, penicillin;
Reveal the answer

Correct: C

"Penicillin" is nonessential info renaming "discovery" — it needs matching fences on both sides. C opens with a dash and closes with a dash.

A smashes the words together. B mixes a colon with a comma — mismatched. D mixes a comma with a semicolon — mismatched, and the semicolon wrongly implies a complete sentence follows.

Stretch

Three factors doomed the expedition: poor planning, inadequate supplies, ___ brutal winter arrived earlier than expected.

  1. ANO CHANGE — comma
  2. Bsupplies; and
  3. Csupplies: and
  4. Dsupplies—and
Reveal the answer

Correct: A

The colon after "expedition" already did its job: complete sentence + drumroll + list. Inside the list, items are separated by plain commas — A is correct. The trap here: students see fancy punctuation nearby and assume the list needs upgrading too. It doesn't — the colon already fired.

B wrongly upgrades a list comma to a semicolon. C stacks a second colon for no reason. D's dash breaks the list structure.

One-line takeaway

Semicolon = period. Colon/dash = drumroll after a complete sentence. Fences must match in pairs.

The trap

Mismatched pairs (open with a comma, close with a dash); upgrading simple list commas to semicolons because it looks more SAT-like.

W3

Subject–verb agreement — the skeleton strip

Standard English Conventions
Must-know

The intuition

The SAT buries the subject under prepositional phrases and asides so your ear grabs the nearest noun instead of the real subject. The can't-unsee-it technique: strip the sentence to its skeleton — cross out prepositional phrases (of ___, in ___, with ___) and parenthetical asides, and the true subject and verb stand naked next to each other.

Original: "The box of chocolates on the kitchen counter ___ missing."

Stripped: "The box of chocolates on the kitchen counter ___ missing."  →  subject = box (singular) → is.

Always singular: each every everyone anyone neither either nobody

Memorize the lineup above — they're singular no matter what follows. And neither…nor verbs agree with the nearer subject.

Simple

The box of chocolates on the kitchen counter ___ missing.

  1. ANO CHANGE — are
  2. Bis
  3. Cwere
  4. Dhave been
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

Strip it: "The box of chocolates on the kitchen counter ___ missing." Subject = box (singular) — so the verb must be singular: is.

A, C, and D all agree with "chocolates" — the decoy noun sitting closer to the verb.

Stretch

Neither the engineers nor the project manager ___ satisfied with the test results, and each of the inspectors ___ filed a separate complaint.

  1. ANO CHANGE — were / have
  2. Bwas / has
  3. Cwere / has
  4. Dwas / have
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

Two agreement points. (1) Neither/nor → the verb agrees with the nearer subject: "manager" (singular) → was. (2) Strip the second: "each of the inspectors ___ filed" — each is always singular → has.

A fails both. C gets the second right but the first wrong (the classic nearer-subject trap). D gets the first right but trips on "each."

One-line takeaway

Cross out the prepositional phrases; the subject left standing must match the verb.

The trap

The decoy noun (nearest noun ≠ subject); neither/nor agreeing with the nearer subject; each/every/everyone always singular.

W4

Verb tense & form consistency

Standard English Conventions
Must-know

The intuition

The SAT tests time logic, not fancy tenses. Rule: don't shift tense unless the timeline shifts. If the sentence lives in the past, every verb lives in the past. Also check that the sentence actually has a main verb — one that starts with "Having…" or "Which…" and never delivers a real verb is a fraud.

The "past-before-past" pattern

Two past events — the earlier one takes had.

"had approved"
earlier event → past perfectpast-perfect
"released"
later event → simple pastsimple past

Read the template into memory: "by the time X happened (past), Y had already happened."

Simple

The researchers collected the samples in June and ___ them to the lab for analysis.

  1. ANO CHANGE — transport
  2. Btransported
  3. Cwill transport
  4. Dhave transported
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

"Collected" sets the timeline: past. The parallel past action needs the past tense → transported.

A, C, and D shift time for no reason — the sentence gives you no later timestamp to justify them.

Stretch

By the time the committee released its report, the city ___ already approved the new zoning law.

  1. ANO CHANGE — has
  2. Bhad
  3. Cwill have
  4. Dhas had
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

Two past events, one before the other: the approval happened before the release. Past-before-past takes the past perfect ("had").

A ("has approved") is present perfect — wrong timeline entirely. C is future. The pattern to internalize: "by the time X happened, Y had already happened."

One-line takeaway

One timeline per sentence — shift tense only when time actually shifts; past-before-past takes "had."

The trap

Present perfect (has/have) sneaking into a purely past-tense sentence; a sentence with no main verb at all.

W5

Pronoun–antecedent clarity

Standard English Conventions
Must-know

The intuition

Every pronoun must point to one clear, specific noun (its antecedent) and match it in number. The SAT's favorite trick: a pronoun with two possible antecedents — grammatically it could point at either, so the sentence is ambiguous and wrong.

Also: they/them/their can't point to a singular noun, and it can't point to a whole idea. When in doubt, the fix is to repeat the noun.

pronoun → one unmistakable noun & same number
Singular on the SAT: committee team company jury

Words like committee and team are singular collective nouns — so "they" can never point at them.

Simple

When Maya handed Priya her notes, ___ was grateful for the help.

  1. ANO CHANGE — she
  2. BMaya
  3. Cthey
  4. Dit
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

"She" could be Maya or Priya — ambiguous, so wrong. B repeats the noun and kills the ambiguity.

C and D don't even make sense — wrong number and wrong kind of thing entirely.

Stretch

The committee praised the volunteers, but ___ noted that the fundraising goal had not yet been met.

  1. ANO CHANGE — they
  2. Bit
  3. Cthe committee
  4. Dhe
Reveal the answer

Correct: C

"They" is technically pointing at "the committee" — but committee is a singular collective noun on the SAT (like team, company, jury). A plural pronoun can't point to a singular noun. C repeats the noun. This is the stretch version because the ambiguity isn't obvious — it's number mismatch hiding behind a reasonable-sounding "they."

A has the number mismatch. B ("it") is cold and awkward pointing at people; the test prefers repeating the noun. D invents a person.

One-line takeaway

Every pronoun needs one unmistakable noun to point to — and must match it in number.

The trap

"They" pointing to singular collective nouns (committee, team, company); two nouns of the same type making it/he/she ambiguous.

W6

Modifier placement

Standard English Conventions
Must-know

The intuition

A describing phrase at the start of a sentence must describe the noun that comes right after the comma. If it doesn't, the modifier is dangling — describing nothing — or misplaced — describing the wrong thing. The fix is always mechanical: put the thing being described immediately after the comma.

Read it literally and laugh at the absurd image: if the rain is suddenly walking to the store, you've found the error.

[describing phrase], + the thing it describes — immediately after the comma

The correct answer often repeats the noun from the modifier ("the achievement…") instead of using a pronoun ("it…"). Watch for that shape.

Simple

Walking to the store, ___ started to rain.

  1. ANO CHANGE — it
  2. Bthe rain
  3. CI got caught as it
  4. Dwe were caught when it
Reveal the answer

Correct: D

"Walking to the store" must describe whoever comes after the comma. A and B make the rain walk to the store — read it literally and laugh. D puts "we" right after the comma: people can walk.

C is grammatically passable but wordy and clunky; D is the clean SAT fix.

Stretch

Named in 1999 as one of the greatest achievements by a US chemist, ___ was Percy Julian's synthesis of cortisone.

  1. ANO CHANGE — it
  2. Bthe achievement
  3. Cchemists celebrated
  4. Dthe honor belonged to
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

Strip to the skeleton: "Named in 1999 as one of the greatest achievements by a US chemist, ___ was Percy Julian's synthesis." The someone/something that was named must come right after the comma. B — "the achievement was Percy Julian's synthesis" — nails it: the achievement is what was named. The real SAT's favorite shape: the correct answer repeats the noun from the modifier instead of using a pronoun.

A lets "it" stand in for the synthesis, leaving the modifier dangling. C and D put the wrong noun after the comma.

One-line takeaway

Whatever the opening phrase describes must come immediately after the comma — read it literally.

The trap

A pronoun (it, they) after the comma that points at the wrong noun; the correct answer repeating the noun instead of using a pronoun.

W7

Parallelism

Standard English Conventions
Nice-to-have

The intuition

Items in a list — or two sides of a comparison — must wear the same grammatical uniform. Noun matches noun, verb matches verb, "-ing" matches "-ing." When the SAT breaks parallelism, one item will be in a different form, and your ear will catch the wobble if you read the list items together, skipping the rest.

Simple

The job requires patience, attention to detail, and ___.

  1. ANO CHANGE — to work well with others
  2. Bworking well with others
  3. Ccollaboration
  4. Dbeing able to work well with others
Reveal the answer

Correct: C

The list so far: "patience" (noun), "attention to detail" (noun phrase), ___. C — "collaboration" (noun) — keeps the uniform.

A, B, and D are infinitive or "-ing" phrases — the wrong uniform, even though the meaning is right.

Stretch

The coach emphasized not only physical conditioning but also ___.

  1. ANO CHANGE — that mental toughness matters
  2. Bdeveloping mental toughness
  3. Cmental toughness
  4. Dto develop mental toughness
Reveal the answer

Correct: C

"Not only X but also Y" is a correlative pair — X and Y must match in form. X = "physical conditioning" (noun phrase), so Y must be a noun phrase: mental toughness.

A is a whole clause, B and D are verb forms — all three break the uniform.

One-line takeaway

List items and "not only/but also" pairs must match in grammatical form — read the items together and listen for the wobble.

The trap

One item smuggled in as an infinitive or clause among nouns; the wobble hidden by a long list.

W8

Apostrophes — possession vs. plural

Standard English Conventions
Nice-to-have

The intuition

Apostrophes do two jobs and two jobs only: possession (the dog's bone = the bone of the dog) and contraction (it's = it is). They never, ever make plurals — "the 1990's" is wrong; "the 1990s" is right.

For possession, the test is mechanical: flip it to an "of" phrase. "The students' lounge" = "the lounge of the students" (plural possessors → apostrophe after the s).

See it's? Expand it to "it is". Sentence still works? ✓ keep the apostrophe.  Breaks? ✗ you want its.

Simple

The ___ coats were hung in the hallway closet.

  1. ANO CHANGE — student's
  2. Bstudents'
  3. Cstudents
  4. Dstudents's
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

Multiple students own the coats → plural possessive → apostrophe after the s: students'.

A is singular possessive (one student). C is just a plural with no possession. D is never correct in standard English.

Stretch

___ going to be difficult for the team to maintain ___ lead in the final quarter.

  1. ANO CHANGE — Its / their
  2. BIt's / its
  3. CIts / its
  4. DIt's / their
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

First blank: "___ going to be" = "it is going to be" → contraction → it's. Second blank: "maintain ___ lead" = "the lead of it (the team)" → possessive, no apostrophe → its.

C and D flip the contraction/possessive. D also uses "their" for the singular collective noun "team" — see W5.

One-line takeaway

Apostrophes = possession or contraction only — expand "it's" to "it is" to check.

The trap

"Its" vs. "it's" — the single most confused pair on the test; apostrophes on plain plurals (1990s, CDs, dogs).

W9

Transitions — name the relationship first

Expression of Ideas
Must-know

The intuition

Transition questions are not vocabulary questions — they're logic questions. The word is just a label for the relationship between two ideas. The protocol:

1. Summarize idea 1 and idea 2 in a few words each. 2. Name the relationship — before looking at the choices: continuation (same direction), contrast (opposite direction), cause–effect, or example/sequence. 3. Pick the word that labels that relationship. Students who match "fancy words" instead of relationships donate these points.

↔ Contrast

however · but · in contrast · nonetheless

Second idea reverses or complicates the first.

→→ Continuation

moreover · also · specifically · in addition

Same direction — more of what was already said.

⇒ Cause–effect

consequently · therefore · as a result · thus

One idea leads to the other.

∴ Example / sequence

for example · for instance · first · finally

An instance of the claim, or a step in order.

Name the relationship from the ideas first, then scan the words — never the other way around.

Simple

Hummingbirds have the highest metabolism of any bird, burning energy at an astonishing rate. ___ they must feed almost constantly, visiting hundreds of flowers each day.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. AHowever,
  2. BFor example,
  3. CConsequently,
  4. DIn contrast,
Reveal the answer

Correct: C

Idea 1: insane metabolism. Idea 2: must eat constantly. Relationship: cause → effect → Consequently.

A and D signal contrast (wrong direction). B signals example — but idea 2 isn't an example of idea 1, it's a result.

Stretch

The new zoning law was intended to increase affordable housing by allowing taller buildings near transit stations. ___ developers have used the law primarily to build luxury apartments, arguing that only high-end units make the construction costs worthwhile.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. AMoreover,
  2. BHowever,
  3. CFor instance,
  4. DSpecifically,
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

Idea 1: law intended to create affordable housing. Idea 2: developers built luxury units instead. The outcome undercuts the intention — that's contrast → However.

The stretch traps: D ("Specifically") is the comfort pick — idea 2 feels like detail about the law, but "specifically" continues the same direction while this sentence reverses it. A ("Moreover") is the same-direction sibling trap. C fails because luxury apartments are not an example of affordable housing.

One-line takeaway

Summarize both ideas, then name the relationship (continue / contrast / cause / example) before you look at the words.

The trap

"Specifically" and "moreover" as comfort picks when the sentence actually reverses direction; near-synonym transitions labeling different relationships.

W10

Rhetorical synthesis — notes questions

Expression of Ideas
Must-know

The intuition

You're given a student's messy notes and a goal ("the student wants to emphasize…"). The correct answer is the choice that achieves the goal using only the notes — nothing added, nothing important dropped. Two checks: (1) does it do what the goal asks? (2) is every claim traceable to the notes? Wrong answers smuggle in outside claims or quietly answer a different goal.

Simple

  • The axolotl is a salamander native to Lake Xochimilco, Mexico.
  • It can regenerate lost limbs and parts of its heart and brain.
  • Wild populations are critically endangered due to habitat loss.

Goal: The student wants to introduce the axolotl to an audience unfamiliar with it, emphasizing its remarkable abilities.

  1. AThe axolotl, a salamander native to Lake Xochimilco, can regenerate lost limbs and even parts of its heart and brain.
  2. BThe axolotl is critically endangered due to habitat loss in Mexico.
  3. CSalamanders are amphibians that live in lakes throughout the world.
  4. DScientists should do more to protect the critically endangered axolotl.
Reveal the answer

Correct: A

Goal = introduce + emphasize remarkable abilities. A introduces (what and where it is) and covers the abilities (regeneration) — both from the notes.

B drops the abilities — wrong goal. C adds an outside claim ("throughout the world") not in the notes. D argues a position — a different goal entirely.

Stretch

  • Study 1 (2021): 8 weeks of meditation reduced participants' self-reported anxiety by 25%.
  • Study 2 (2023): 8 weeks of meditation reduced cortisol (a stress hormone) levels by 12%, but self-reported anxiety fell only 5%.
  • Both studies used adult beginners with no prior meditation experience.

Goal: The student wants to present the findings of the two studies, noting a discrepancy between them.

  1. ATwo studies of adult beginners found that 8 weeks of meditation reduced anxiety and cortisol, proving meditation eliminates stress.
  2. BWhile a 2021 study found meditation reduced self-reported anxiety by 25%, a 2023 study found only a 5% drop in self-reported anxiety despite a 12% drop in cortisol.
  3. CMeditation is more effective than medication for treating anxiety in adults.
  4. DCortisol levels dropped 12% in a 2023 study of experienced meditators.
Reveal the answer

Correct: B

Goal = present both findings + note the discrepancy. B gives both studies' numbers, and the discrepancy is explicit: big anxiety drop vs. small anxiety drop alongside a cortisol drop.

A adds "proving meditation eliminates stress" — extreme and not in the notes. C compares to medication — never studied. D twists "adult beginners" into "experienced meditators" — the one-word trap — and drops Study 1.

One-line takeaway

Match the goal, use only the notes — every claim must be traceable, nothing added.

The trap

Choices that achieve a different goal; one-word twists (beginners → experienced); extreme conclusions ("proves," "eliminates").

W11

Precision & concision

Expression of Ideas
Must-know

The intuition

When several choices are grammatically fine, the SAT prefers the one that says the thing clearly and without waste. Redundancy ("the end result," "repeat again," "the reason is because") is always wrong.

But — and this is the stretch insight — shortest is not automatically right: the answer must still be complete and precise. A shorter choice that drops needed meaning loses to a longer one that keeps it.

Simple

The committee's ___ decision was announced on Friday.

  1. Afinal outcome of the
  2. Bultimate end
  3. Cdecision
  4. Dfinal decision that was made
Reveal the answer

Correct: C

"Decision" already means a final outcome — C is one clean word saying the whole thing.

A, B, and D pad it with redundancy: "final outcome," "ultimate end," "decision that was made."

Stretch

The biologist spent three years tracking the wolves, and her data ___ that pack territories shift with prey migration.

  1. Aclearly and unmistakably demonstrated beyond any doubt
  2. Bsuggested
  3. Cindicated
  4. Dproved conclusively
Reveal the answer

Correct: C

A and D are redundant/overstated ("clearly and unmistakably demonstrated beyond any doubt"; "proved conclusively" — and "proved" overstates what tracking data can do). Between B and C, both are concise, but "suggested" understates three years of data — "indicated" is the precise verb for what data does.

The lesson: concision serves precision — never sacrifice the exact meaning to save a word.

One-line takeaway

Cut redundancy ruthlessly — but the winner is the clearest and most precise choice, not merely the shortest.

The trap

Redundant pairs (advance forward, end result); a shorter choice that drops essential meaning.

Test day · Must-know

Adaptive module strategy for R&W

The digital SAT adapts: Module 1 mixes difficulties, and your performance routes you to an easier or harder Module 2. A harder Module 2 is how you access the top score bands. The protocol is mechanical — follow it.

1

Module 1 is the gateway

Take it seriously from question 1 — a strong Module 1 unlocks the harder (higher-ceiling) Module 2. Don't "warm up" into the test.

2

Every question counts — none can be skipped strategically

No penalty for wrong answers, so never leave a blank — guess on anything you're stuck on.

3

Budget ~71 seconds per question

32 minutes for 27 questions a module. If one question passes 2 minutes, flag it and move — the easy questions later are worth just as much.

4

Use the tools

Bluebook's annotation and highlight are free. Cross out eliminated choices with the strikethrough tool — it makes elimination visual and deliberate.

5

Don't get psyched out by difficulty

If Module 2 feels harder, that's good news — it means Module 1 went well. Stay steady; those questions are worth more.

6

Trust the built-in order

The section orders itself: Craft & Structure → Information & Ideas → grammar → Expression of Ideas. If grammar is your strength, bank those points fast and buy time for the harder reading questions.

One-line takeaway

Crush Module 1 to unlock the hard Module 2, keep a 71-second pace, and never leave a question blank.
